Last week we talked about keeping a stored battery alive. This week I want to talk about the other side of a garage that sits quiet for weeks at a time: keeping it from catching fire.

Most garage fires do not start with a bang. They start with something small and ordinary — a fuel hose that has gone soft with age, a wire rubbing through where nobody looks, a pile of oily rags left in a corner. By the time anyone notices, the small thing has become the whole garage.

The good news is that almost all of it is preventable, and none of it takes expensive equipment. It takes a good extinguisher where you can reach it, a little discipline about fuel and rags, and a habit of looking at the wiring before you turn the key.

## The Extinguisher by the Door

Start here, because this is the one item that has to be right before anything goes wrong.

For a home garage with classic cars, the extinguisher you want is a dry-chemical ABC unit — it handles ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids like gasoline, and electrical fires, which covers just about everything a garage can throw at you. A 5-pound unit is a sensible size for a home garage: big enough to matter, small enough that you will actually grab it and use it. The one I would point you to is the [Kidde Pro 340](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F4F4978J/?tag=fitzgeraldcla-20) — a rechargeable 5-pound ABC unit rated 3-A:40-B:C, with a gauge you can check at a glance and a wall hanger in the box.

Mount it by the door you would leave through, at a height you can reach without stretching, and keep it visible. An extinguisher buried behind boxes, or sitting on the floor under a workbench, is decoration. The point is that you can put a hand on it on your way out, without going deeper into the garage to get it.

A few habits to go with it:

- Check the pressure gauge once a month. If the needle has dropped out of the green, the unit needs service or replacement.
- Turn it upside down and give it a few firm taps now and then, so the dry chemical inside does not settle and pack down.
- If you ever use it, even for a few seconds, have it recharged or replaced. A partly used extinguisher is not a reliable one.
- Know its limits. An extinguisher is for a fire that has just started and is still smaller than you are. If the fire is growing, or the smoke is thick, get out and call for help. No car is worth going back in for.

One more thing: never use water on a fuel fire or an electrical fire. Water spreads burning gasoline and conducts electricity. That is the one rule worth teaching everyone who ever walks into your garage.

## Fuel: Store Less, Store It Right

Gasoline is the most dangerous thing most of us keep in the garage, and we tend to treat it like it is harmless because we handle it every week.

Keep only what you need, in an approved container made for fuel — metal is my preference — with the cap tight and the container sitting on the floor, not on a shelf above a water heater or a furnace. Store it away from any ignition source: pilot lights, heaters, welders, and the battery charger on the bench all count.

A garage attached to the house deserves extra care. Fuel vapors are heavier than air and they travel along the floor, looking for a spark. Keep the storage area ventilated, keep containers out of the living space, and never store fuel in the same room as a gas appliance if you can avoid it.

For a car that will sit, the fuel in the tank needs attention too. Old gasoline goes stale and leaves varnish behind, and a tank that sits half-full breathes in moist air as temperatures change. And a habit that costs nothing: after you work on a fuel line, a carburetor, or a fuel pump, look under the car before you start it. A drip you catch on the floor is a repair. A drip you miss is a fire waiting for a hot exhaust manifold.

## Oily Rags: The Fire That Starts Itself

This is the one that surprises people, and it is the one I take most seriously in my own garage.

Rags soaked in linseed oil, oil-based stain, or the oil and solvents from cleaning parts can catch fire on their own, with no spark at all. As the oil oxidizes it gives off heat, and a folded pile of rags holds that heat in until it reaches ignition temperature. It happens in a quiet garage, in the middle of the night, with nobody working.

The fix is simple and it is a habit, not a purchase:

- Never leave oily rags in a pile, in a trash can, or wadded up on the bench.
- Lay them flat outdoors to dry, spread out so the heat can escape, away from the house and anything flammable.
- Once they are fully dry and stiff, they are safe to throw away.
- Or keep a metal container with a tight lid for them — a proper oily-waste can — and empty it regularly.

If you remember one line from this whole article, make it this one: a pile of oily rags is not clutter. It is a fire that has not started yet.

## Wiring: The Quiet Fault

A classic car's wiring is where most garage fires actually begin, and it is the hardest place to see the problem coming.

Old wiring fails quietly. Insulation gets brittle and cracks. A wire chafes through where it passes over a sharp edge or through a bulkhead. A terminal works loose and starts arcing. A previous owner splices in an accessory with a twist of tape and no fuse, and it sits there for years drawing current through a wire that was never meant to carry it.

None of that announces itself. The car still starts. The lights still work. And then one day a shorted wire gets hot enough to light the insulation around it.

A few things I check on my own cars, and I would suggest you do the same:

- Look at the wiring where it moves or rubs — around the steering column, through the firewall, along the frame. Chafed insulation is the most common fault I find.
- Feel the battery cables and the main connections. A connection that is warm after a drive is a connection with resistance, and resistance makes heat.
- Check that every added circuit — a modern radio, an electric fan, auxiliary lights — has its own fuse, sized for the wire, and placed close to the power source. An unfused wire is a fuse made out of the car itself.
- If a fuse blows, find out why before you put a bigger one in. A bigger fuse does not fix a short. It just lets the wire get hotter before anything stops it.
- Keep a battery disconnect in mind for a car that sits. Cutting power at the battery, as I described in last week's piece on [battery tenders and disconnects](/post/battery-tenders-disconnects-electrical-storage), takes a whole family of electrical faults off the table while the car is parked.

If you want to check for a fault properly, a good multimeter is the tool. ## A Short Checklist Before You Walk Away

Fire safety in a garage is mostly a set of small habits, done the same way every time. Mine looks like this:

- Extinguisher mounted by the exit door, gauge in the green, reachable without thinking
- Fuel in approved containers, caps tight, away from ignition sources, and only as much as I need
- Oily rags laid flat to dry or in a lidded metal can — never in a pile
- Battery disconnected or on a smart maintainer when the car sits, never on an old trickle charger left unattended
- A look under the car after any fuel work, and a look at the wiring wherever it rubs
- Nothing plugged in that does not need to be, and no charger or heater running in an empty garage

It takes about two minutes. That is a cheap price for a garage full of cars that cannot be replaced.

## The Goal

A garage fire is not usually bad luck. It is usually a small fault that went unnoticed, or a small habit that went undone, for a long time.

The cars in my garage are American heritage, and they are also irreplaceable in the ways that matter — the history, the hands that worked on them, the miles behind them. A five-pound extinguisher by the door, fuel stored like the hazard it is, and rags handled like the risk they are: that is how those cars stay in the garage instead of becoming a story about the garage that used to be there.
