Last week we talked about [keeping the garage from catching fire](/post/fire-safety-classic-car-garage). This week I want to talk about the other thing that has to be right before you ever slide underneath a car: what holds it up while you are under there.

A jack lifts the car. That is all a jack does. It is not a stand, it is not a support, and it is not something to trust with your life. Every year somebody gets hurt because a car came off a jack — a seal blew, a valve stuck, a handle got bumped — and there was nothing else between them and several thousand pounds of old American steel.

The system is three pieces, and they work together: a jack to lift, stands to hold, and chocks to keep the car from moving. Get all three right and working under a car is ordinary work. Skip any one of them and you are gambling.

## The Jack: Low Enough to Get Under the Car

The first problem with a classic car is getting the jack under it at all. My '66 Corvette sits low, and a tall steel jack will not slide under the frame without scraping, or will not reach the lifting point at all. That is why the jack in my garage is a low-profile one.

The one I use is the [Arcan ALJ3T 3-ton aluminum low-profile floor jack](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0047D2FWM/?tag=fitzgeraldcla-20). It drops down to 3.75 inches, which gets under the '66 without a fight, and lifts to 18 inches, which is enough for stock-height classics. Three tons covers one end of the Galaxie or the Nash with room to spare, and at around 57 pounds it is light enough to carry around the garage — a steel jack the same size runs closer to 85.

Full disclosure, since you should always know where you stand here: those are my Amazon listings, and if you buy through them it helps keep this site running, at no extra cost to you. I only list gear I would put on my own cars.

The honest trade-off on this jack is the top end of the lift. Eighteen inches is the lowest maximum lift of the premium jacks, so if you are working on a lifted truck it will not get the wheels high enough. For a stock-height classic it is the right tool. Buy the jack for the cars you actually own, not the ones you might own someday.

A couple of habits with any floor jack:

- Lift on the frame or on a solid crossmember — never on a floor pan, a fuel line, or a suspension arm.
- Pump it up slowly and watch the car as it rises. If it starts to shift or lean, set it back down and reposition.
- Once the car is up, the jack's job is done. Get the stands under it and lower the car onto them. Never leave a car sitting on the jack while you work underneath it.

## The Stands: Size Them to the Car, and Buy the Pin

Jack stands are where people try to save money, and it is the worst place in the garage to do it. A stand is a simple thing — a ratchet, a pawl, and a steel post — and the difference between a good one and a cheap one is whether there is a second thing holding the load when the first thing fails.

Both stands I recommend have a double lock: a ratchet pawl plus a removable steel locking pin. If the pawl ever gets bumped or slips, the pin is still there with steel under the load. That pin is the whole reason I recommend these.

For the Corvette and the Nash, the [BIG RED Torin T43002A 3-ton double-locking jack stands, pair](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BQ2S32NX/?tag=fitzgeraldcla-20) are the right size. They adjust from 11-1/4 to 16-3/4 inches, with a wide pyramid base that does not want to tip. Three tons is the rating for the pair — a ton and a half per stand — which covers either end of a classic car with margin.

For the Galaxie, and for anyone working higher under a frame car, I keep a heavier pair as well: the [BIG RED Torin T46002A 6-ton double-locking jack stands, pair](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00026Z3DQ/?tag=fitzgeraldcla-20). They run from 15-3/8 to just under 24 inches, so they hold the car higher and carry far more weight.

One thing to check before you pair a tall stand with a low jack: the stand's minimum height has to be lower than the jack's maximum lift, or you will never get the car high enough to set the stand. The 6-ton stands start at 15-3/8 inches and the Arcan tops out at 18, so it works — with little to spare. If your jack only lifts to 15 inches, those tall stands are decoration. Measure before you buy, not after.

And the maker's own limit is worth repeating: the 3-ton pair is not for big pick-ups or large off-road SUVs. Stands are rated per pair, and the rating is the whole pair, not each stand. Size them to the car you are lifting, with margin, and if you are between sizes, go up.

## Where the Stands Go on an Old Frame

This is the part that matters most on a car from the sixties, because the frame is not always where you expect it, and it is not always as solid as it looks.

On a frame car — the Galaxie, the Nash — set the stands under the frame rails, on flat, solid sections, as close to the wheels as you can get them. On a car with a subframe or unibody sections, use the factory lifting points in the owner's manual, or the reinforced pinch welds, and spread the load with a saddle that fits.

A few rules I do not bend on:

- Never set a stand under a floor pan, a rocker panel that has gone soft, or a suspension arm that moves. The stand will punch through or slip, and it will do it while you are underneath.
- Work on flat concrete, never on gravel, dirt, or a sloped driveway. A stand on soft ground sinks. A stand on a slope walks.
- Check the frame where the stand will sit before you lift. Rust, a previous repair, or a dented rail changes the answer. If the metal is thin or flaking, pick another spot or fix the frame first.
- Set both stands at the same height, lower the car onto them slowly, and then give the car a firm shake before you go under. It should sit solid and silent. If it rocks or creaks, lift it back up and reset.
- Keep the jack nearby, under the car but not carrying weight, as a backup — never as the support.

## The Chocks: The Cheapest Safety in the Garage

Chocks are the piece people skip because they look too simple to matter. They are two rubber wedges, and they are the only thing stopping a car from rolling off the stands if the parking brake lets go or the ground is not quite level.

I use the [MaxxHaul 70472 solid rubber wheel chocks, 2-pack](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01CGU14T2/?tag=fitzgeraldcla-20). They are eight inches long, solid rubber, heavy enough to stay put, and they grip instead of sliding. The pair costs less than a tank of gas, and they are the difference between a car that stays where you put it and one that does not.

The habit is simple: chock the wheels that stay on the ground, on both sides of the tire, front and back, before you lift anything. If you are lifting the front, chock the rear wheels. If you are lifting the rear, chock the front. A parking brake alone is not a chock — brakes fail, cables stretch, and a car on stands with no chocks is a car that can roll.

For larger truck or trailer tires, step up to a bigger chock — the eight-inch size suits car tires. Match the chock to the tire, the same way you match the stand to the car.

## A Short Checklist Before You Go Under

Working under a car is safe when the system is complete. Mine looks like this:

- Car on flat concrete, parking brake set, transmission in park or in gear
- Wheels on the ground chocked on both sides before lifting
- Jack on the frame, lifted slowly, car watched as it rises
- Stands under solid frame or factory points, same height, pin locked in
- Car lowered onto the stands, jack eased off, firm shake test before going under
- Nothing and nobody under the car while it is on the jack alone — not for a second, not for a quick look

It takes about five minutes. That is a cheap price for coming out from under the car the same way you went in.

## The Goal

A car in the air is a heavy thing held up by simple tools, and simple tools only work when they are used as a system. A low jack that actually fits under the car, stands sized to the car with a pin backing up the ratchet, chocks on the wheels that stay on the ground, and the five minutes of habits in between — that is the whole job.

The cars in my garage are American heritage, and they are also irreplaceable in the ways that matter — the history, the hands that worked on them, the miles behind them. They are worth holding up properly.
